Executive Summary

UK urban low-voltage networks were dimensioned for a domestic load profile that electrification is now changing faster than the assets serving it. This document sets out the business requirements for the Dynamic Resilient Grid (DRG): a demand-side analytics capability that tells a network planner which neighbourhoods are approaching their historical demand envelope, how often and for how long they exceed it, and how much worse electric vehicle and heat-pump adoption will make it.

The business problem in one paragraph

Distribution planners today rely on historical averages and diversified design figures. These are adequate for steady-state planning but silent on the questions electrification actually raises. A planner can say what average demand was last winter; they cannot say how many hours per week a given street will sit above its historical peak envelope once forty per cent of its households charge an electric vehicle. The consequence is reinforcement that is either premature - capital spent early on assets that were not yet constrained - or late, which is worse, because lead times for distribution reinforcement are measured in years.

The proposed capability

DRG forecasts half-hourly neighbourhood demand from smart-meter data, defines a stress threshold statistically from each neighbourhood's own history, simulates electric-vehicle and heat-pump adoption from the bottom up, and reports the resulting change in peak demand, stress frequency and stress duration. It explains its own outputs, replays history as a live feed to demonstrate operational use, and exposes everything through a dashboard and an API.

Evidence from the pilot implementation

A working implementation has been built and run end to end. The figures below are measured outputs, not projections. They were produced on a calibrated reference dataset rather than the licensed extract, so absolute values will differ in production; the capability they demonstrate does not.

What is being asked for

Approval of the business requirements in Section 7, the acceptance criteria in Section 13, and the resourcing implied by the scope in Section 4. The technical design that satisfies these requirements is documented separately in DRG-SAD-001.

One boundary the sponsor must understand before approving DRG is a demand-side statistical capability. It has no network topology, no transformer or cable ratings and no voltage model, because the available smart-meter data contains none of these. It identifies where and when to look, and quantifies how much electrification worsens the picture. It does not, and cannot, replace an engineering assessment of a specific asset. Any business case built on its outputs must respect that boundary.

Business Context and Problem Statement

Market and regulatory drivers

Drivers creating the need
DriverImplication for local distribution networks
Net Zero commitmentsTransport and heat are being electrified simultaneously, moving load onto networks that were sized before either was contemplated.
Electric vehicle adoptionA 7 kW domestic charger is roughly five times the after-diversity demand of the household it is attached to, and it typically switches on in the evening, when the network is already at its busiest.
Heat pump deploymentElectrified heating is weather-driven: it peaks precisely on the coldest evenings, which is also when the underlying domestic peak is highest.
Smart-meter rolloutHalf-hourly consumption data now exists at scale, making a demand-side analytical approach possible for the first time without new network instrumentation.
Regulatory efficiency pressurePrice-control frameworks reward evidence-led investment. A defensible, data-driven case for the timing of reinforcement has direct regulatory value.

The problem

Three specific gaps exist in current practice, and each maps to a business requirement in Section 7.

  1. No local, temporal view of exceedance. Planners know average and peak demand. They do not know the frequency and duration with which a specific neighbourhood exceeds its own historical envelope, which is the quantity that determines whether a constraint is emerging or already present.
  2. No quantified link between adoption and stress. Adoption forecasts exist and network capacity is known in aggregate, but the function connecting the two - how many additional stressed hours per week does the next ten per cent of EV uptake create - is not routinely computed at neighbourhood granularity.
  3. No explanation attached to the numbers. A forecast that cannot say why it expects a peak is difficult to defend in an investment committee and impossible to challenge constructively.

Cost of inaction

Business Process: As-Is and To-Be

Why BR-03 is worded as it is The requirement deliberately asks for a definition that does not depend on asset data. The finding with the clearest commercial implication Electric vehicles alone at 80% adoption raise the peak by 154%; heat pumps alone at 70% raise it by 113%. Together they raise it by 237% - 29 percentage points less than the sum of the two. The loads peak at overlapping but not identical times, so treating them as additive overstates the combined constraint. Planning that adds separately-derived EV and heat-pump uplifts will therefore over-specify reinforcement.

After Diversity Maximum Demand		The peak electrical demand attributable to a single connected customer or device once the fact that not everyone uses power simultaneously is taken into account. The standard quantity UK distribution planners use for sizing.
Adoption elasticityThe marginal change in local peak demand produced by each additional tranche of technology uptake, expressed here per ten percentage points.
DNODistribution Network Operator - the licensed operator of a regional low- and medium-voltage electricity network.
Diversity factorThe reduction applied to a sum of individual peak demands to reflect that they do not all occur at the same instant.
Half-hour periodThe settlement granularity of the GB electricity market; 48 periods per day.
Heating degreesDegrees Celsius by which outside temperature falls below a reference balance point; the driver of electrified heating demand.
Low Carbon LondonA smart-meter trial conducted in London, published through the UK Data Service as study 7857, providing half-hourly household consumption for 2011-2014.
LV feederLow-voltage circuit supplying a cluster of domestic properties from a distribution substation.
NeighbourhoodIn this capability, a fixed-size group of smart-meter households used as the unit of analysis in place of a physical feeder, which the anonymised source data does not identify.
Peak amplificationThe percentage increase in a neighbourhood's maximum half-hourly demand under an electrification scenario, relative to the un-electrified base case.
Pre-emptive alertAn alert raised because the forecast crosses the stress threshold, before measured demand has done so. The only alert type that provides lead time.
Promotion gateThe automated quality decision that determines whether a retrained model is allowed to replace the one currently in use.
Stress escalationThe multiple by which stress frequency increases under a scenario, relative to the base case.
Stress eventA contiguous run of two or more half-hours in which demand equals or exceeds the stress threshold. The unit in which duration statistics are reported.
Stress periodA single half-hour in which demand equals or exceeds the stress threshold.
Stress thresholdThe 95th percentile of a neighbourhood's own historical half-hourly demand. A statistical reference point, not an asset rating.
